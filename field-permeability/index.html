Field Permeability Testing (Lefranc / Lugeon) in Augusta Georgia

Knowledgeable. Thorough. Resourceful.

LEARN MORE

Augusta Georgia sits on the Fall Line where the Piedmont crystalline rocks meet the Coastal Plain sediments, creating a sharp contrast in hydraulic conductivity. The sandy terraces along the Savannah River can yield permeabilities of 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ m/s, while the deeper saprolite often drops to 10⁻⁶ m/s or lower. For any excavation or dam project in this zone, a field permeability test (Lefranc / Lugeon) is essential to measure actual in-situ flow rather than relying on lab estimates on disturbed samples. We perform these tests in boreholes or test pits using constant-head or falling-head procedures depending on the expected transmissivity, and the results feed directly into dewatering design, grouting programs, and slope stability models. Before mobilizing the crew we often review existing geotechnical drilling data to correlate the test horizons with the local stratigraphy.

In Augusta Georgia, the Fall Line contrast can produce a 1000-fold change in permeability across a single borehole — field testing is the only reliable way to capture it.

Scope of work

The underlying geology of Augusta Georgia is dominated by the Fall Line transition, meaning a single borehole can pass from residual Piedmont clay into clean Coastal Plain sand within a few meters. We design each Lugeon test stage for a 1 m to 3 m interval, applying pressures of 0.1 to 1.0 MPa to measure both laminar and turbulent flow components — the Lugeon value (liters per minute per meter at 1 MPa) directly indicates grout take. For the Lefranc variant, we use constant-head in pervious soils and falling-head in low-permeability clays, recording time-drawdown curves to compute k in cm/s. A typical test sequence in Augusta Georgia includes:
  • Three ascending pressure steps for Lugeon tests to detect hydraulic fracturing or joint dilation
  • Stabilization criteria of ±5% flow rate over 10 minutes
  • Water temperature and viscosity correction to 20°C
This procedure aligns with ASTM D5084 for flexible-wall permeameters and ASTM D4630 for in-situ borehole tests. The results are then compared to the consolidation characteristics of the foundation soils to predict long-term seepage behavior under load.

Area-specific notes

A common mistake we see in Augusta Georgia projects is assuming that lab permeability tests on small Shelby tube samples represent the in-situ mass behavior. Fissures, root holes, and sand lenses in the Piedmont saprolite can increase bulk conductivity by orders of magnitude compared to the intact matrix. Designers who skip the field permeability test (Lefranc / Lugeon) often face unexpected inflows during excavation, requiring emergency dewatering that delays the schedule by weeks. The Savannah River alluvium is particularly tricky — its lateral variability means a single test point may miss a preferential flow path unless the test program is tied to a detailed geological model.

Q&A

What is the difference between a Lefranc test and a Lugeon test?

The Lefranc test is designed for soils and measures hydraulic conductivity under constant or falling head at low pressure (typically <0.1 MPa), while the Lugeon test is used in rock and applies multiple pressure steps up to 1.0 MPa to assess both permeability and the tendency for joint dilation or hydraulic fracturing. In Augusta Georgia, we often run Lefranc tests in the upper Coastal Plain sands and switch to Lugeon when the borehole reaches the Piedmont saprolite or fractured gneiss.

How much does a field permeability test cost in Augusta Georgia?

For a typical project in the Augusta area, the cost ranges between US$710 and US$1,160 per test interval. This includes mobilization of the double-packer system, water supply, pressure monitoring, and a written report with k values and Lugeon units. The final price depends on borehole depth, number of intervals, and site access conditions — we always provide a fixed quote after reviewing the borelog.

How many test intervals are recommended for a dam foundation in the Piedmont?

For a dam foundation on the Piedmet saprolite and gneiss typical of Augusta Georgia, we recommend at least one Lugeon test per 3 m of borehole in the upper 15 m, and one per 5 m below that. If the rock exhibits open joints or weathered zones, the interval spacing should be reduced to 1.5 m to capture localized high-permeability features. The total number of tests is always balanced against the project risk and the variability observed during drilling.

Can you run field permeability tests in existing monitoring wells?

Yes, provided the well screen is isolated with a packer and the casing is clean. For existing wells in Augusta Georgia that were not designed for packer testing, we may need to use a straddle-packer assembly or inflatable packers to seal against the casing wall. The test results are still valid for estimating bulk permeability, but the interval length is limited by the screen length of the well. We typically recommend a test duration of 30 to 60 minutes per stage.
